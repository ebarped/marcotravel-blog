# Galifornia: Three ways to experience summer in Galicia

We are always haunted by the same old cliché: that it rains all year round in Galicia, that summer here is just a three-day mirage. But those of us who live here know the truth. When the heat kicks in, this land turns into a kind of tailor-made paradise that we might as well call <strong style="color: #ff7f50ff;">Galifornia</strong>: white sandy beaches that look like the Caribbean, sunsets that stay etched in your memory, and a daylight that lingers until ten at night. The difference is that here, instead of avocado, there is <strong style="color: #ff7f50ff;">pulpo a feira</strong>, and instead of waves and Californian surfers, there are mussel rafts floating in every estuary.

The problem is not a lack of plans in Galicia, the problem is that Galicia is infinite, and trying to cover it all in just a few days is an impossible mission. So you don't go crazy trying to figure out an impossible map, we have prepared three routes with three completely distinct souls: islands and estuaries, wild coast with lighthouses, or inland vineyards. Choose yours and if you prefer <strong style="color: #ff7f50ff;">Marco</strong> to help you turn it into an itinerary, you can generate your personalized itinerary for Galicia in minutes.

## Plan 1: A weekend of islands, hórreos, and the best sunset of your life

This is a way to make the most of a weekend in the <strong style="color: #ff7f50ff;">Rías Baixas</strong> without complications: a day on an island, a second day between Pontevedra and Combarro, and a sunset in Bueu to cap off the trip in style.

### Day 1: Ons or Cíes

Both islands are part of the <strong style="color: #ff7f50ff;">Parque Nacional Illas Atlánticas</strong>, and that implies an important condition worth keeping in mind: between <strong style="color: #ff7f50ff;">May 15 and September 15</strong> access is limited by law, with a daily visitor quota and mandatory authorization in addition to the boat ticket. It is not a recommendation, it is a requirement; in the middle of August it is best to request authorization on the [official website of the Xunta de Galicia](https://autorizacionillasatlanticas.xunta.gal) at least a week in advance.

Cíes can be reached from Vigo, Cangas, or Baiona; Ons, from Bueu, Portonovo, or Sanxenxo, with a crossing of about 40 minutes and fewer crowds than its older sister. Whatever your choice, dedicate the full day to it: beach, trail, and a leisurely walk, with no other plans on the agenda.

If you leave from Vigo, take the opportunity to explore <strong style="color: #ff7f50ff;">O Berbés</strong> before getting into the car. It is the seafaring neighborhood right next to the port, the fishing heart of the city for centuries that still preserves that essence, with traditional taverns where you can have dinner and quietly enjoy a glass of wine. If you set off from Bueu or Portonovo, a walk along the port and dinner at a local seafood restaurant serves the exact same purpose: wrapping up the day nicely before the second leg.

### Day 2: Pontevedra, Combarro, and sunset in Bueu

Start in Pontevedra, taking your time. Its old town is one of the best-preserved in Galicia, and moving from square to square: <strong style="color: #ff7f50ff;">Praza da Leña, Praza da Ferrería, Praza da Verdura</strong> is almost a game, as each one has its own distinct atmosphere. Along the way, drop by the <strong style="color: #ff7f50ff;">Igrexa da Peregrina</strong>, with its curious scallop-shell floor plan, one of the most recognizable images of the center. If you feel like grabbing a bite, the Praza da Verdura area brings together several traditional tapas bars. Save your real appetite for what comes next.

Continue toward <strong style="color: #ff7f50ff;">Combarro</strong> and have lunch there. Its granaries are built right on top of the rocks, so close to the waterline that at high tide they look like they are floating. After eating, a stroll through the narrow stone streets, among stone crosses and fishermen's houses, is a great way to digest before heading toward Bueu.

![Hórreos of Combarro in Rías Baixas by the sea](https://thumb.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/e/e0/Combarro_-_Pontevedra_4.jpg/960px-Combarro_-_Pontevedra_4.jpg?utm_source=commons.wikimedia.org&utm_campaign=imageinfo&utm_content=thumbnail)

To close out the day, a moment you shouldn't miss: the sunset at Tuia or Portomaior. Neither of the two beaches should be rushed, so arrive with time to spare. At Portomaior, <strong style="color: #ff7f50ff;">Chiringuito Carmela</strong> is a safe bet, with good company and a table facing the sea. At Tuia, more secluded and less crowded, <strong style="color: #ff7f50ff;">Chiringuito Galifornia</strong> is famous for its mojitos. Both offer dinner service and, on some summer nights, live concerts while the sun bids farewell over the estuary. Whatever your choice, the sunset is the best way to close the day. Nothing more is needed.

## Plan 2: The Lighthouse Route, a stretch well-lived

The <strong style="color: #ff7f50ff;">Ruta de los Faros</strong> spans about 200 km along the <strong style="color: #ff7f50ff;">Costa da Morte</strong>, uniting lighthouses, cliffs, and fishing villages into one of Galicia's most spectacular coastal itineraries. Doing the whole thing in a single day is not the best idea; the way to truly enjoy it on a getaway is to choose a section and travel it calmly.

A good starting point is the area of <strong style="color: #ff7f50ff;">Muxía and Fisterra</strong>. The <span style="color: #FF7F50; font-weight: bold;">Faro de Fisterra </span>, which for centuries marked what was believed to be the end of the world, and the sanctuary of <strong style="color: #ff7f50ff;">A Barca</strong> in Muxía, with the rocks that tradition associates with the legend of the Virgin, are mandatory stops along this stretch. If you prefer fewer crowds and equally breathtaking landscapes, the section toward <strong style="color: #ff7f50ff;">Cabo Vilán</strong>, in Camariñas, with its lighthouse and cliffs, is another good option.

![Faro de Cabo Vilan](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/9/9e/Faro-vilan-3875989_1920.jpg/960px-Faro-vilan-3875989_1920.jpg?utm_source=commons.wikimedia.org&utm_campaign=imageinfo&utm_content=thumbnail)

Whichever section you choose, do it on foot. It is the best way to genuinely enjoy the landscape, and along the way you earn the hearty feast waiting for you afterward.

For lunch, in Fisterra a good option is <strong style="color: #ff7f50ff;">Restaurante O Centolo</strong>, right next to the port with sea views, specializing in local fish and seafood. If your section has been Camariñas, near the port you will find simple seafood restaurants offering fresh catch of the day. And if you fancy something different from fish, in either of the two towns you will also find spots with good Galician beef and <span style="color: #FF7F50; font-weight: bold;">pulpo á feira</span>, the other unmissable culinary highlight of the area. If what you are looking for are views you won't easily forget, head toward <strong style="color: #ff7f50ff;">Ézaro</strong>: the Xallas River waterfall drops almost straight into the sea, and its terraces offer panoramic views that merit a mandatory stop.

The key to Plan 2 is not completing the 200 km, but rather leaving you eager to come back next year for the next section.

## Plan 3: Ribeira Sacra, the other Galician summer

If the first two plans taste like the sea, the <strong style="color: #ff7f50ff;">Ribeira Sacra</strong> is the inland Galician summer: canyons carved out by the Sil and Miño rivers, heroic vineyards on impossible terraces, and monasteries that have been part of the landscape for centuries.

The best way to get to know the area is from the water, with a <span style="color: #FF7F50; font-weight: bold;">catamaran ride through the Sil Canyon</span>, which allows you to see from below what from land can only be guessed at: rock faces of over 500 meters and vineyards clinging to the slope almost vertically. They depart from various docks, such as Doade or the Belesar recreation area, and it is best to book in advance during high season.

On land, two stops shape the day: the <strong style="color: #ff7f50ff;">Monasterio de Santo Estevo</strong> (now a Parador), with its cloisters from different eras and a very unique silence of the place, and a viewpoint in the Cabezoás or Vilouxe area are good options to see the riverbanks from above, with the vineyards drawing lines across the canyon.

![Cañón del Sil y viñedos de la Ribeira Sacra](https://st5.depositphotos.com/2071605/71639/i/600/depositphotos_716395828-stock-photo-landscape-sil-river-ribeira-sacra.jpg)

And since you are in a vineyard area, it is worth doing a tasting at a winery producing <span style="color: #FF7F50; font-weight: bold;">Mencía or Godello</span>, the two varieties that define Ribeira Sacra. For lunch, <strong style="color: #ff7f50ff;">A Faragulla</strong>, in the historic center of Chantada, is a safe recommendation: well-prepared Galician product cuisine in a simple and close environment, perfect to wrap up a plan as Galician and summery as the other two, even if it has little coastline.
