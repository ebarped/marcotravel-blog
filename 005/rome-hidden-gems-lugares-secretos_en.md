## Hidden courtyards and secret views

### Galleria Sciarra, the frescoed courtyard two steps from Trevi

A minute from the crowds throwing coins into the Trevi Fountain, a barely-marked doorway on Via Piombino opens onto one of the most beautiful courtyards in Rome: the **Galleria Sciarra**. Its walls are covered in Art Nouveau frescoes painted at the end of the 19th century, depicting the virtues of women in flowing, Belle Époque style. It is free, it is quiet, and almost nobody goes in — the perfect answer when the Trevi is three people deep.

![Frescoed courtyard of Galleria Sciarra, a hidden gem in Rome](005/assets/rome-hidden-gems-galleria-sciarra.jpg)

### The Aventine keyhole, three countries in a single glance

On the Aventine hill, outside the door of the **Priory of the Knights of Malta**, a short queue forms in front of a green door. Look through the keyhole and the view snaps into focus: a perfectly trimmed hedge-lined tunnel ending in the dome of **St. Peter's Basilica**. In one glance you cross three sovereign territories — the Knights of Malta, Italy, and the Vatican. It is free and open day and night, and a two-minute walk from the **Orange Garden (Giardino degli Aranci)** if you time it with sunset.

![The Aventine keyhole framing the dome of St. Peter's](005/assets/rome-aventine-keyhole.jpg)

## Churches with masterpieces you can almost keep to yourself

### San Luigi dei Francesi, three free Caravaggios

Four hundred years after he scandalized Rome, Caravaggio still stops you cold. In the **Contarelli Chapel** of the French church of San Luigi dei Francesi, a five-minute walk from Piazza Navona, hang three of his most intense paintings of the life of St. Matthew, including *The Calling of Saint Matthew*. Entry is free; slip a coin into the machine to light them for fifteen minutes.

### Santa Maria della Vittoria, Bernini's Ecstasy in a near-empty church

While the queues for the Sistine Chapel wind around the block, one of the greatest sculptures in the history of art sits in a small Baroque church that is almost always empty. In the **Cornaro Chapel**, Bernini's *Ecstasy of Saint Teresa* (1647–52) shows the saint in a swoon of marble so fluid it seems to float, lit by a window the artist deliberately concealed. It is free, and the chapel around it is staged like a theatre.

### Basilica di San Clemente, three layers of two thousand years

A short walk from the Colosseum, the **Basilica of San Clemente** is an archaeological layer cake. The upper level is a radiant 12th-century basilica; below it lies a 4th-century church with medieval frescoes; and below that, a 1st-century Roman house and a temple to the god Mithras, with an underground stream still flowing beneath. One building, two thousand years, and barely any of the Colosseum's crowds. The upper church is free; entry to the underground levels costs €10.

![Golden mosaics of the Basilica di San Clemente, Rome](005/assets/rome-san-clemente.jpg)

## Pockets of peace and a different Rome

### The Non-Catholic Cemetery, where the poets lie

Beneath cypress trees next to the Pyramid of Cestius lies one of the most beautiful and romantic cemeteries in the world — a walled garden where **John Keats** and **Percy Bysshe Shelley** are buried. Shelley himself called it "the most beautiful and solemn cemetery I ever beheld", and four years later he was laid to rest in it. A colony of cats has lived here since the 1850s. Entry is free, with a suggested donation.

### Parco degli Acquedotti, the Roman countryside by metro

A short metro ride from the center on **Metro Line A** to Giulio Agricola station — renamed "Parco degli Acquedotti" in 2025 after the park — the **Parco degli Acquedotti** spreads across nearly 600 acres of green where the monumental arches of the ancient Aqua Claudia and Anio Novus still stand. Seven aqueducts rise in partial ruin over open fields, and at golden hour the light turns the crumbling stone amber. It feels like walking through the Roman countryside — except you are still inside Rome.

![Arches of the ancient aqueducts at Parco degli Acquedotti, Rome](005/assets/rome-parco-acquedotti.jpg)

### Centrale Montemartini, ancient statues inside a power plant

One of Rome's strangest and most satisfying contrasts hides inside a decommissioned early-20th-century electricity plant. At **Centrale Montemartini**, a satellite of the Capitoline Museums, Greek and Roman sculptures gathered from across ancient Rome are displayed against colossal diesel engines and turbines — industrial machinery and marble-cold ancient gods side by side. Far quieter than the main museums, it is the sort of place that feels like a discovery of your own.

## A fairy-tale quarter and a neighborhood with taste

### Quartiere Coppedè, Gaudí's little brother in Rome

Half an hour from the center, the **Quartiere Coppedè** is a tiny enclave of fantastical buildings designed by Gino Coppedè in the early 1900s, mixing Art Nouveau, Baroque, and Gothic around the **Piazza Mincio**. You enter beneath a grand arch with an iron chandelier hanging over the street; at the heart of it stands the **Fountain of the Frogs**. The Beatles even went for a swim there after a concert at the nearby Piper Club in 1965.

![The fairy-tale architecture of Quartiere Coppedè, Rome](005/assets/rome-quartiere-coppede.jpg)

### The Jewish Ghetto, Rome's oldest neighborhood of taste

Established in 1555, the **Jewish Ghetto** is one of the oldest in Europe and one of the city's most atmospheric corners. Wander the porticoes of the ancient **Portico d'Ottavia**, admire the Great Synagogue, and sit down for *carciofi alla giudia* — crisp, golden, Jewish-style fried artichokes that are a genuine Roman institution.
