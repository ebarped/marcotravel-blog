# Two Camino de Santiago routes to discover Galicia differently

Talking about the Camino de Santiago as if there were only one Camino would be a bit of an understatement. You can reach Santiago by following the coast, crossing mountains, walking through large cities or connecting small villages where you might end up greeting more cows than people.

And that is exactly part of the charm.

Although the French Way is the best-known route, there are many other Camino de Santiago routes that <strong style="color: #ff7f50ff;">offer completely different experiences</strong>. Some are quieter, others more demanding, and some simply have that something that makes you start reading out of curiosity and end up thinking: “Right, how many days do I need to do this?”

For this article, we have chosen two routes that we particularly like.

First, an alternative to the traditional Portuguese Way: the <strong style="color: #ff7f50ff;">Spiritual Variant</strong>, which crosses the Rías Baixas and even allows you to complete part of the route by boat. Then there is the <strong style="color: #ff7f50ff;">Primitive Way</strong>, much more mountainous, demanding and closely connected to the origins of the pilgrimage to Santiago.

Two very different Caminos.

One has the sea, vineyards and coastal villages. The other has mountains, elevation gain and enough climbs to make you reconsider a few life decisions.

Let’s go.

## Spiritual Variant of the Portuguese Way: monasteries, sea and vineyards

The Portuguese Way is one of the best-known routes to Santiago. But when you reach Pontevedra, <strong style="color: #ff7f50ff;">a very interesting alternative appears</strong>: temporarily leaving the traditional route and continuing along the Spiritual Variant of the Portuguese Way.

And the name already sounds promising.

The route continues through Poio, Combarro, A Armenteira and Vilanova de Arousa before reconnecting with the Camino towards Santiago. What makes this variant so interesting is everything it manages to include in relatively few days: <strong style="color: #ff7f50ff;">historic centres, fishing villages, monasteries, forests, old watermills, vineyards and, finally, the Arousa estuary</strong>.

<div aria-hidden="true" style="display:block; height:36px; min-height:36px; line-height:36px; font-size:1px;">&nbsp;</div>

### From Pontevedra to Armenteira via Combarro

The route begins by leaving Pontevedra behind and heading towards Poio before reaching one of the most recognisable places in the Rías Baixas: <strong style="color: #ff7f50ff;">Combarro</strong>. Even if you have never been there, you have probably seen a photo. Narrow stone streets, traditional houses, granaries standing almost right beside the sea and the estuary constantly in the background. It is the perfect place to stop and enjoy its narrow streets and local cuisine. Combarro is also known for its large number of hórreos, traditional rural granaries typical of north-western Spain, especially Galicia and Asturias, historically used to store and dry food such as grain and corn while protecting it from ground moisture and animals.

Then it is time to change gears.

The route gradually leaves the coast behind and begins climbing towards <strong style="color: #ff7f50ff;">A Armenteira</strong>, one of the more demanding sections of this first part of the route. There you will find the Monastery of Santa María de Armenteira, one of the highlights of this variant. In just a few hours, you go from walking beside the sea to finishing surrounded by nature and mountains.

And there is still plenty more to come.

### Ruta da Pedra e da Auga

The next section includes one of the most special parts of the entire Spiritual Variant: the <strong style="color: #ff7f50ff;">Ruta da Pedra e da Auga</strong>.

The name is fairly self-explanatory.

From Armenteira, the path begins descending alongside the river, surrounded by vegetation, bridges, stone and old watermills. For several kilometres, you walk almost constantly accompanied by the sound of flowing water. The route has a low to medium-low difficulty level. It is one of Galicia’s most popular and magical trails, ideal for families, children or older people who are used to walking.

Then the landscape changes again.

Small villages begin to appear, together with the vineyards so characteristic of O Salnés, and little by little the estuary comes back into view. The route continues towards <strong style="color: #ff7f50ff;">Vilanova de Arousa</strong>. And this is where perhaps the most unusual part of the entire Camino begins.

<div aria-hidden="true" style="display:block; height:36px; min-height:36px; line-height:36px; font-size:1px;">&nbsp;</div>

### Walking the Camino de Santiago… by boat?

Yes.

Well, part of it.

From Vilanova de Arousa, it is possible to take the <strong style="color: #ff7f50ff;">Traslatio Route</strong>, a boat journey through the Arousa estuary and along the Ulla River linked to the Jacobean tradition. The boat reaches the Pontecesures area and, from there, the Camino continues on foot towards Padrón. So technically, you can say that you have completed part of the Camino de Santiago by boat.

The journey lasts approximately an hour and a half. However, departure times depend strictly on the tides. Another particularly interesting detail for people travelling with animals is that this section is completely pet-friendly and allows pets to travel on board with their owners.

There is also a land alternative for anyone who prefers to complete the entire route on foot, but the boat journey is precisely one of the features that makes this variant so different. The Spiritual Variant of the Portuguese Way is a particularly interesting option if you want to combine the Camino with nature, coastline, heritage and gastronomy without necessarily committing to a route lasting several weeks.

## Primitive Way: going back to the origins of the Camino de Santiago

Now the scenery changes completely.

This time we leave the sea behind for a while and head towards the mountains. The <strong style="color: #ff7f50ff;">Primitive Way</strong> begins in Oviedo and crosses the interior of Asturias and Galicia before reaching Santiago de Compostela. And its name has a fairly simple explanation: it is considered the oldest Camino de Santiago route.

Its origins are connected to the earliest pilgrimages to Compostela during the Middle Ages. More than a thousand years later, thousands of pilgrims still follow this route.

Although probably with better shoes these days.

<div aria-hidden="true" style="display:block; height:36px; min-height:36px; line-height:36px; font-size:1px;">&nbsp;</div>

### A Camino shaped by the mountains

If the Spiritual Variant is defined by the sea and the Rías Baixas, the Primitive Way <strong style="color: #ff7f50ff;">has another very obvious main character: the mountains</strong>. As soon as you leave Oviedo, elevation changes, forests, rural paths and small settlements begin to appear.

And it is worth saying clearly: this is not exactly the easiest Camino.

One of its best-known sections is the <strong style="color: #ff7f50ff;">Hospitales Route</strong>, a mountain alternative that crosses some of the most spectacular landscapes of the Asturian section. But looking only at the distance can be a little misleading. The specific high-mountain section of Hospitales covers around 15 kilometres, while the complete stage from Borres to Berducedo is approximately 24.8 to 26 kilometres long.

However, elevation matters here. A lot.

Weather conditions can also play their part, especially at higher altitudes. For these reasons, this section is considered highly difficult and is widely regarded as the queen stage and the most demanding part of the entire Jacobean route. In return, the Primitive Way offers something that is becoming increasingly difficult to find on some of the busiest routes: a sense of isolation, nature and many kilometres where the path itself becomes the main attraction.

### Entering Galicia through A Fonsagrada

After crossing Asturias comes one of those moments that makes any Camino special: entering Galicia.

The Primitive Way enters Galicia through the <strong style="color: #ff7f50ff;">A Fonsagrada</strong> area and from there continues towards places such as O Cádavo, Castroverde and finally Lugo. These first Galician stages keep the rural and mountainous character that defines the Primitive Way: small villages, forests, trails and plenty of green.

Reaching Lugo also marks an interesting change.

After several stages dominated by small rural settlements, you suddenly arrive in a city with more than two thousand years of history and a Roman wall that forms part of its urban landscape. Walking through it during the Camino is one of those moments you remember. From Lugo, the terrain gradually becomes gentler until reaching <strong style="color: #ff7f50ff;">Melide</strong>, where the Primitive Way finally joins the French Way.

And from there, the atmosphere changes quite a bit.

More pilgrims, more activity and the final shared stages towards Arzúa, O Pedrouzo and, finally, Santiago de Compostela.

<div aria-hidden="true" style="display:block; width:100%; height:2px; line-height:2px; font-size:1px; background-color:#eadfce; margin:52px 0;">&nbsp;</div>

## So… Spiritual Variant or Primitive Way?

As we say in Galicia: it depends.

The <strong style="color: #ff7f50ff;">Spiritual Variant of the Portuguese Way</strong> can be a fantastic option if you want to combine the Camino with villages, gastronomy, coastline and nature without necessarily committing to an overly long or complicated route. Combarro, Armenteira, the Ruta da Pedra e da Auga and the boat journey through the estuary make almost every stage feel different.

The <strong style="color: #ff7f50ff;">Primitive Way</strong>, on the other hand, asks for a little more. It is a route for people who enjoy walking, are not too afraid of elevation and want an experience more closely connected to mountains, rural landscapes and the origins of the Camino de Santiago.

One keeps looking towards the sea. The other towards the next mountain.
