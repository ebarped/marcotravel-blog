# Two Camino de Santiago routes to discover Galicia differently

Talking about the **Camino de Santiago** as if there were only one Camino would be a bit of an understatement.

You can reach Santiago by following the coast, crossing mountains, walking through large cities or connecting tiny villages where you might end up greeting more cows than people.

And that is exactly part of the charm.

Although the French Way is the best-known route, there are many other <strong style="color: #ff7f50ff;">Camino de Santiago routes</strong> offering completely different experiences. Some are quieter, others more demanding, and some simply have that *something* that makes you start reading out of curiosity and end up thinking: “Right, how many days do I need to do this?”

For this article, we have chosen two routes that we particularly like.

First, an alternative to the traditional **Portuguese Way**: the **Spiritual Variant**, which crosses the Rías Baixas and even allows you to complete part of the route by boat.

Then there is the **Primitive Way**, much more mountainous, challenging and closely connected to the origins of the pilgrimage to Santiago.

Two very different Caminos.

One has the sea, vineyards and coastal villages.

The other has mountains, elevation gain and enough climbs to make you reconsider a few life decisions.

Let’s go.

## Spiritual Variant of the Portuguese Way: monasteries, sea and vineyards

The **Portuguese Way** is one of the best-known routes to Santiago.

But when you reach Pontevedra, an interesting alternative appears: temporarily leaving the traditional route and continuing along the <strong style="color: #ff7f50ff;">Spiritual Variant of the Portuguese Way</strong>.

The name already sounds promising.

The route continues through Poio, Combarro, A Armenteira and Vilanova de Arousa before reconnecting with the Camino towards Santiago.

What makes this route particularly interesting is everything it manages to include in just a few days: historic centres, fishing villages, monasteries, forests, old watermills, vineyards and, finally, the Arousa estuary.

Quite a complete menu.

### From Pontevedra to Armenteira via Combarro

The route begins by leaving Pontevedra behind and heading towards Poio before reaching one of the most recognisable places in the Rías Baixas: **Combarro**.

Even if you have never been there, you have probably seen a photo.

Narrow stone streets, traditional houses, granaries standing almost right beside the sea and the estuary constantly in the background.

Not exactly a bad place to stop for a while.

Then it is time to change gears.

The route gradually leaves the coast behind and begins climbing towards **A Armenteira**, one of the more demanding sections of this part of the Camino.

There you will find the **Monastery of Santa María de Armenteira**, one of the highlights of the Spiritual Variant.

In just a few hours, you go from walking beside the sea to finishing surrounded by nature and mountains.

And there is still plenty more to come.

### Ruta da Pedra e da Auga

The next section includes one of the most special parts of the entire **Spiritual Variant**: the **Ruta da Pedra e da Auga**.

The name is fairly self-explanatory.

From Armenteira, the path begins descending alongside the river, surrounded by vegetation, bridges, stone and old watermills.

For several kilometres, you walk almost constantly accompanied by the sound of flowing water.

It is one of those places where you take out your phone for one photo, then another, then another because the light is better… and suddenly realise you have spent half an hour moving at tortoise speed.

Then the landscape changes again.

Small villages begin to appear, together with the vineyards so characteristic of O Salnés, and little by little the estuary comes back into view.

The route eventually reaches **Vilanova de Arousa**.

And this is where perhaps the most unusual part of the entire Camino begins.

### Walking the Camino de Santiago… by boat?

Yes.

Well, part of it.

From Vilanova de Arousa, you can take the **Traslatio Route**, a boat journey through the Arousa estuary and along the Ulla River linked to the Jacobean tradition.

The boat reaches the Pontecesures area, from where the Camino continues on foot towards Padrón.

So technically, you can say you completed part of the Camino de Santiago by boat.

Not a bad story to tell afterwards.

There is also a land route for anyone who prefers to walk the entire way, but the boat journey is precisely one of the things that makes this route so different.

The <strong style="color: #ff7f50ff;">Spiritual Variant of the Portuguese Way</strong> is a particularly good choice if you want to combine the Camino with nature, coastline, heritage and food without necessarily committing to a route lasting several weeks.

It is also a great excuse to discover a part of Galicia that deserves to be explored slowly.

## Primitive Way: going back to the origins of the Camino de Santiago

Now the scenery changes completely.

We leave the sea behind for a while and head towards the mountains.

The **Primitive Way** begins in Oviedo and crosses the interior of Asturias and Galicia before reaching Santiago de Compostela.

And its name has a fairly simple explanation: it is considered the <strong style="color: #ff7f50ff;">oldest Camino de Santiago route</strong>.

Its origins are linked to the earliest pilgrimages to Compostela during the Middle Ages.

More than a thousand years later, thousands of pilgrims still follow this route.

Although probably with better shoes these days.

### A Camino shaped by the mountains

If the Spiritual Variant is defined by the sea and the Rías Baixas, the **Primitive Way** has another very obvious main character: the mountains.

As soon as you leave Oviedo, elevation changes, forests, rural paths and small settlements begin to appear.

And there is something worth saying clearly.

This is not exactly the easiest Camino.

One of its best-known sections is the **Hospitales Route**, a mountain alternative that crosses some of the most spectacular landscapes of the Asturian section.

But looking only at the distance can be a little misleading.

Here, elevation matters. A lot.

The weather can also play its part, especially at higher altitudes.

In return, the Primitive Way offers something that is becoming harder to find on some of the busiest routes: isolation, nature and long stretches where the path itself becomes the main attraction.

### Entering Galicia through A Fonsagrada

After crossing Asturias comes one of those moments that makes any Camino special: entering Galicia.

The **Primitive Way enters Galicia through the A Fonsagrada area** and from there continues towards places such as O Cádavo, Castroverde and eventually **Lugo**.

These first Galician stages keep the rural and mountainous character that defines the Primitive Way: small villages, forests, trails and plenty of green.

It is Galicia, so that last part should not come as a surprise.

Reaching Lugo also marks an interesting change.

After several stages dominated by rural landscapes and small settlements, you suddenly arrive in a city with more than two thousand years of history and a Roman wall that is part of its everyday urban landscape.

Walking through it during the Camino has to be one of those moments you remember.

From Lugo, the terrain gradually becomes gentler until reaching **Melide**, where the Primitive Way finally joins the French Way.

And from there, the atmosphere changes quite a bit.

More pilgrims, more activity and the final shared stages towards Arzúa, O Pedrouzo and, finally, Santiago de Compostela.

## So… Spiritual Variant or Primitive Way?

It depends on what you are looking for.

And probably also on how your legs are feeling.

The **Spiritual Variant of the Portuguese Way** can be a fantastic option if you want to combine the Camino with villages, food, coastline and nature without necessarily committing to a very long route.

Combarro, Armenteira, Ruta da Pedra e da Auga and the boat journey through the estuary make almost every section feel different.

The **Primitive Way**, on the other hand, asks for a little more.

It is a route for people who enjoy walking, are not too afraid of elevation and want an experience more closely connected to mountains, rural landscapes and the origins of the Camino de Santiago.

One keeps looking towards the sea.

The other towards the next mountain.

One even lets you stop walking for a few hours and get on a boat.

On the other, you will probably look at a climb at some point and think:

**“Did we really need to go up there?”**

Probably.

And once you reach the top, you will probably understand why.
