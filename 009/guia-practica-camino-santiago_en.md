# Practical Camino de Santiago Guide: how a pilgrimage is prepared

If you have already read our article on the Spiritual Variant and the Primitive Way, you will know that the Camino de Santiago is not a single route but many, and that choosing between them is only the first step. The next question is the one everyone ends up asking: <strong style="color: #ff7f50ff;">so how do I organise it now?</strong>

How many days I need, what I put in my backpack, where I sleep and how much money I am going to spend. These are practical questions, and this article answers them all.

The good news: the Camino is much easier to prepare than it looks. You do not need to be a mountain expert or carry impressive technical gear. What you need is clear information, a light backpack and a decision about how many days you want to walk.

> **Key Takeaways**
>
> - To earn the Compostela you must walk the final 100 kilometres on foot (or 200 by bike) and carry a stamped credential, with at least two stamps per day on the final stretch.
> - Hostels cost between 8 and 15 euros a night, and the pilgrim menu of the day costs around 10-15 euros. A realistic budget is 30 to 40 euros a day.
> - The ideal backpack weighs 5 to 8 kilos: two changes of clothes, rain gear, boots or trail shoes already broken in, and little else.
> - The best seasons are spring and autumn. In summer there are more pilgrims, more heat and you should book beds in advance.
> - Starting in Sarria (French Way) lets you walk the final 100 kilometres in 5 to 7 days, the most popular format.

## First things first: the credential and the Compostela

Before thinking about stages or packing, there is one procedure that shapes everything: the pilgrim credential.

The credential is an official document that gets stamped at each stage (at hostels, churches, bars or town halls) and proves that you are walking the Camino. You get it at hostels, parishes and Camino associations, and you can also buy it before you start.

Why does it matter so much? Because without it, there is no Compostela.

The <strong style="color: #ff7f50ff;">Compostela is the certificate awarded to those who complete the pilgrimage</strong> for religious or spiritual reasons. To earn it, the rule is to walk at least the final 100 kilometres (or cycle the last 200 by bike) and collect the corresponding stamps. On the final stretch, it is recommended to get at least two stamps a day to prove your route.

This explains why so many people start in Sarria: from there to Santiago is about 100 kilometres, exactly the minimum distance, and it can be completed in a week without rushing.

## How to plan your stages

Once you have chosen your route, it is time to turn the kilometres into days.

The usual pace is between 20 and 25 kilometres a day, although it is best to start with shorter stages and build up gradually. An average pilgrim takes 5 to 7 days to cover the final 100 kilometres from Sarria, and 4 to 5 weeks to walk the full French Way.

When planning, it helps to know the typical stages of your chosen route and the villages with hostels that let you stop when the time comes. Two tips that save headaches:

- **Build in margin.** Plans hold up until a climb, a blister or a village you fall in love with appears. Planning one short stage too many is better than chaining three long ones.
- **Book in summer.** In high season hostels fill up. Booking a bed for the next day is standard practice and avoids ending the day looking for somewhere to sleep.

The Camino can be walked alone or with company. If you go alone, do not worry: the route is well signposted and someone always appears to share stretches with. If you go in a group, the most comfortable option is for each person to walk at their own pace and meet up at the end of the stage.

## What to pack

The golden rule of the Camino is simple: <strong style="color: #ff7f50ff;">what you do not carry, does not weigh</strong>.

A well-prepared backpack weighs between 5 and 8 kilos, water included. If yours is over 10, you are probably carrying too much. This is the base list we recommend:

- **Footwear.** Boots or trail shoes already broken in before you leave. Breaking in new shoes on the Camino is the fastest way to end up with blisters.
- **Clothes.** Two changes of underwear and socks (good quality, technical fibre), two shirts, one pair of trousers and one pair of shorts or leggings, a fleece and a windbreaker. Clothes get washed daily, there is no need to carry more.
- **Rain.** A lightweight poncho or rainproof jacket is essential: Galicia is unpredictable and rain is part of the plan. If yours covers the backpack, even better.
- **Basic first aid.** Plasters for blisters, antiseptic, painkillers, sunscreen and lip balm. Pharmacies are in the villages, a hospital kit is not needed.
- **Documents.** Credential, ID and some cash. Most hostels have accepted cards for a while, but cash is still king in small villages.
- **Extras worth it.** Trekking poles, a small water bottle or reusable flask, earplugs (appreciated in shared hostels) and a compact toiletries bag.

And a reminder: the hostel gives you a bed, a shower and a kitchen, so a lightweight sleeping bag or liner is enough. You do not need a big towel, a hairdryer or half of what you feel like putting in the backpack.

## How much the Camino costs

The Camino remains one of the most affordable trips in Europe, and that is part of its magic.

A typical pilgrim's daily spend breaks down like this:

- **Accommodation:** between 8 and 15 euros a night at public or private hostels. Public ones (municipal or association-run) are the cheapest; private ones offer more comfort and sometimes twin rooms.
- **Food:** the pilgrim menu of the day costs around 10-15 euros. Breakfast and snacks are cheap if you shop at supermarkets.
- **Extras:** coffees, stamps, activities and the odd food treat.

All things considered, <strong style="color: #ff7f50ff;">a realistic budget is between 30 and 40 euros a day</strong>. The final 100 kilometres from Sarria can be done, with this plan, for around 200-250 euros excluding travel to and from the start.

If you want to spend less, public hostels, cooking in the shared kitchens and walking in low season bring the figure down noticeably. If you prefer more comfort, private rooms and hotels with backpack transfer services raise the budget, but also the quality of rest.

## When to go and final tips

The best seasons for the Camino are <strong style="color: #ff7f50ff;">spring (April to June) and autumn (September to October)</strong>: pleasant temperatures, fewer crowds and green landscapes. Summer is high season: more pilgrims, more heat and a need to book. Winter is for experienced walkers, especially on mountain routes like the Primitive Way.

Three tips to close this guide:

- **Break in your feet, not your shoes.** Walk for several weeks beforehand with the footwear and backpack you will use. Blisters are prevented, not cured on the go.
- **Start early.** Leaving early gives you margin for heat, stops and surprises. Hostels fill on a first-come, first-served basis in high season.
- **Enjoy more than you complete.** There is no prize for the longest stage. There is a prize for arriving in Santiago still wanting to repeat.

## Frequently asked questions about preparing the Camino

### Do I need experience or prior fitness?

You do not need hiking experience. With basic fitness and progressive stages, anyone used to walking can do the final 100-kilometre stretch. That said, it is worth training in the weeks before: long walks with the backpack on.

### Can the Camino be done alone?

Yes, and it is one of the most recommendable experiences. The route is very well signposted, hostels create a pilgrim atmosphere and it is common to find company for stretches. With a prepared backpack and clear stages, going alone is easier than it seems.

### How long does it take from Sarria and from the start of the French Way?

From Sarria to Santiago is about 100 kilometres, covered in 5 to 7 days walking roughly 20 kilometres a day. The full French Way, from Saint-Jean-Pied-de-Port, is around 780 kilometres and takes 4 to 5 weeks.
