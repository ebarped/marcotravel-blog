# Christmas Markets in Europe: 3 routes to pick yours

There is an image that shows up every year around this time and makes you want to travel almost by reflex: a cobbled square, warm lights, wooden stalls, the smell of cinnamon and mulled wine, and an entire town determined to make December taste better than the rest of the year.

European Christmas markets are exactly that.

They are also a perfect plan for anyone who wants to travel without complications: the routes are short, the dates are clear, and each city turns its historic centre into a stage built for strolling.

In this article we have put together <strong style="color: #ff7f50ff;">3 Christmas market routes across Europe</strong> so you can choose according to your style: the closest one with village charm, the most traditional one and the most monumental one. Each with its cities, key dates and some tips so you only have to worry about the important part: which stall to hit first.

> **Key Takeaways**
>
> - The Alsace route (Strasbourg and Colmar) is the most compact: fairytale villages and a great festive atmosphere.
> - The German route (Nuremberg and Rothenburg) is the most traditional, with centuries-old markets and classic stalls.
> - The Vienna-Prague route combines two of Europe's most monumental markets, perfect for a city break.
> - Most markets open between late November and 24 December, although many stay open into the first days of January.
> - You can comfortably cover all three routes in 3 or 4 days and combine them with cities, food and museums.

## Why travel to a Christmas market?

European Christmas markets work because they are not just a place to buy decorations.

They are a perfect excuse to wander through historic centres that also look better than ever. Each city places its stalls in the main squares, lights up the streets, sets up ice-skating rinks and plays music on every corner. The result is a plan that works for couples, families, groups of friends and solo travellers alike.

The logistics are simple too.

Distances between markets are short, trains usually connect the cities well, and since the dates are predictable (almost everything happens between late November and Christmas), you can plan ahead without surprises.

There is an extra advantage: <strong style="color: #ff7f50ff;">December has cheaper flights and hotels than summer</strong>, as long as you book in advance. It is one of the few times of the year when you can visit iconic European cities without summer crowds and with an atmosphere that simply does not exist at any other time of year.

## Route 1: Alsace, the fairytale village route

If you are after the most idyllic image of European Christmas, Alsace is your place.

This region in north-eastern France concentrates some of the most beautiful markets on the continent, and its villages look designed for a Christmas card: half-timbered houses, narrow streets, decorations everywhere and markets spread across every square.

The classic route combines two cities very close to each other: <strong style="color: #ff7f50ff;">Strasbourg and Colmar</strong>.

Strasbourg calls itself the Capital of Christmas and it is not far off. Its main market is set up around the cathedral and in Place Kléber, where the famous giant fir tree stands. The whole city joins in: more than 300 stalls across the centre and an atmosphere that starts in late November.

Just a few minutes away by train is Colmar, which many consider the most beautiful village in France at Christmas. Its market spreads the stalls across several historic districts, with Petite Venise as the most photographed area. It is smaller than Strasbourg's, but its charm is very hard to beat.

And if you want more, the area is full of Christmas villages: Riquewihr, Kaysersberg, Ribeauvillé or Turckheim. You can visit them by car or by public transport from Strasbourg. They are the perfect plan if you have an extra couple of days.

**How many days you need:** 3 or 4 if you want to add small villages; 2 if you stick to the two cities.

## Route 2: Germany, the traditional route

If there is one country that takes Christmas markets seriously, it is Germany.

Here the tradition goes back centuries and the stalls stay true to the original format: wood, warm lights, handcrafted products, sausages, gingerbread and glühwein (spiced mulled wine) served in the souvenir mug that everyone ends up collecting.

The perfect starting route is <strong style="color: #ff7f50ff;">Nuremberg, with a stop in Rothenburg ob der Tauber</strong>.

Nuremberg's market is one of the most famous in Europe. It takes place in the Hauptmarkt, the main square, and its origins go back to the 17th century. It is known as the Christkindlesmarkt, and its official opening is led by the Christkind, a character in a golden dress who kicks off the season. The stalls only sell handcrafted or Christmas products, which turns the stroll into a return to a more traditional Christmas.

Less than an hour away by train is Rothenburg ob der Tauber, one of the best-preserved medieval towns in Germany. Its market is more intimate, but the whole town wraps itself in festive atmosphere, and its Käthe Wohlfahrt decoration shop, open all year round with a permanent Christmas setup, is worth the visit.

If you want to extend the route, Munich and its market in Marienplatz are about two and a half hours from Nuremberg by train. It is a huge market with an ice-skating rink and a big-city atmosphere. With that, you have a full week.

**How many days you need:** 2 or 3 for Nuremberg and Rothenburg; 5 if you add Munich.

## Route 3: Vienna and Prague, the monumental route

This route is for anyone who wants to combine Christmas with two of Europe's most spectacular cities.

<strong style="color: #ff7f50ff;">Vienna and Prague are just 4 hours apart by train</strong> and both hold two of the most photographed Christmas markets on the continent.

Vienna's main market sits in front of the city hall, in Rathausplatz, with dozens of stalls, an ice-skating rink and an atmosphere that blends tradition with the festive spirit of a great capital. Around it there are other smaller, specialist markets: Belvedere, Freyung square or Spittelberg, one of the local favourites, with artisan stalls among narrow lanes.

Prague, for its part, sets up its market in the Old Town Square, with the astronomical clock and the towers of the Týn church in the background. The result is one of the most photogenic markets in Europe. The Castle Square has its own smaller, less crowded market too.

The best part of this route is that the journey itself is very simple: trains between both cities are frequent and comfortable, and both cities work very well for exploring on foot.

**How many days you need:** 2 per city, or 4 if you travel at a relaxed pace.

## Which route is best for you?

It depends on what you are looking for, and the good news is that all three options work.

Choose <strong style="color: #ff7f50ff;">Alsace</strong> if you want fairytale villages, compact markets and the chance to combine city with scenery in a few days. It is the shortest route and the one that works best for a first European Christmas experience.

Choose <strong style="color: #ff7f50ff;">Germany</strong> if you care about tradition in its purest form: centuries-old markets, handcrafted products and the Christmas format all others draw from.

Choose <strong style="color: #ff7f50ff;">Vienna and Prague</strong> if you want monumental cities, postcard markets and a trip where Christmas is almost a bonus on top of two destinations worth visiting at any time of year.

One note for those who prefer to avoid crowds: the markets fill up mostly on weekends and from mid-December. Travelling midweek, in early December or right after opening makes the experience noticeably calmer.

## Frequently asked questions about Christmas markets

### When do European Christmas markets open?

Most open between late November (usually the last weekend of the month or the first Friday of December) and close on 24 December. Some, like Strasbourg's or Vienna's, stay open into the first days of January. Exact dates are published a few months ahead on each city's official website.

### How many days do I need for a Christmas market trip?

With 3 or 4 days you can comfortably do any of the three routes. With 5 or 6 you can add small villages or a third city. This type of trip does not need many days because the core of the experience is walking around.

### Is December worth the trip or is it too crowded?

It is worth it, but it pays to pick the dates carefully. December weekends and the days before Christmas Eve are the busiest. In early December, midweek, the atmosphere is complete and the crowds are much smaller.
